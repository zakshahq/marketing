How to block adult content on Android — every method compared

Four ways to do it, from a free setting to a paid app. Each one has a real limit — here's what each covers, what it costs, and what a kid can switch back off.

01Free DNS filtering

Android has a built-in setting called Private DNS (Settings → Network & internet → Private DNS, or Connections → More connection settings on Samsung). Point it at a family-safe DNS resolver and the phone routes its lookups through a filter that blocks known adult and gambling domains — on Wi-Fi and mobile data, since it's a device-wide setting rather than a per-network one.

It's free and takes one screen to set up.

Where this stops. It lives in the same Settings menu a curious teenager already knows how to reach. Turning it off is a few taps, with nothing to stop them and no way for you to find out it happened.

02Google Family Link

Family Link is Google's free supervision app for Android. Its screen-time tools are genuinely good — app limits, bedtime schedules, location, approval requests for new app downloads. For content, it turns Google SafeSearch on or off and lets you block or allow specific sites in Chrome.

Where this stops. No category filtering, and nothing outside Chrome — a kid who opens Firefox or types a URL into an app is unscreened. Supervision also isn't permanent: from age 13 a child can ask to leave it, and once it's gone, so is every setting attached to it.

03Router and carrier controls

Most home routers ship with a parental-control panel, and some mobile carriers offer a family-safe browsing add-on. Both work by filtering traffic at the network level — the router for Wi-Fi in the house, the carrier for its own SIM.

Where this stops. They sit at the front door. Step onto a friend's Wi-Fi, a school network, or plain mobile data from a different provider, and the filtering doesn't come along. For a phone that leaves the house — which is most phones — this covers a fraction of where the browsing actually happens.

FAQ

Questions, answered.

Is free DNS filtering enough on its own?

For a younger child who isn't looking to get around it, yes — it's a real filter for free. For an older kid who knows their way around Settings, it's a five-tap fix with nothing to alert you and nothing to stop them trying again.

Why doesn't Family Link block porn better?

It was built around Google's own apps — SafeSearch and Chrome's site lists — not general category filtering. A browser outside Chrome, or an app with its own browsing, isn't covered.

What happens to router controls on mobile data?

Nothing — they don't apply. Router rules only run on that router's Wi-Fi, so the moment a phone switches to mobile data or another network, filtering stops.

Is Zaksha tamper-proof?

Only in one specific setup: a phone enrolled fresh, before a child uses it, through the device-owner install on the Get the app page — that one can't be uninstalled from the phone. The standard install on a phone already in use is tamper-evident: if it's switched off or removed, you're alerted on WhatsApp and push immediately, not tamper-proof.
